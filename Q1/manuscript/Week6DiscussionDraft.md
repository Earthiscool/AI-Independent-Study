4 Discussion

4.1 Why the Pilot Study Found No Effect

The most important lesson from this project is that dropout only helped once the models had a chance to overfit. In the pilot study, the models trained for only 10 epochs and were still improving when training ended, so there was little overfitting for dropout to prevent. In that situation, dropout mainly slowed learning down, which explains why the dropout models in the pilot performed slightly worse. In the final experiment, models without dropout reached their best validation loss after about 15 epochs and then began to overfit, and this is the situation in which dropout showed a clear benefit.

This means that the training budget can change the conclusion of a dropout experiment. Liu et al. similarly found that the effect of dropout depends on the stage of training at which it is applied.<sup>8</sup> If I had reported only the pilot study, I would have concluded that dropout does not help on Fashion-MNIST, when the real problem was that the experiment ended before dropout could matter. Studies that compare regularization methods should therefore make sure the models are trained long enough to overfit, for example by using early stopping instead of a short fixed number of epochs.

4.2 Dropout Prevents Overfitting and Improves Accuracy

The final experiment showed that dropout reduced overfitting and improved test accuracy at every training size. The exploratory measures in Section 3.4 show the overfitting effect most clearly: at epoch 20, dropout reduced the gap between training and validation accuracy in every one of the 80 seed pairs, and after reaching their best epoch, models with dropout got much less worse than models without dropout. These results match the explanation given by Srivastava et al.<sup>3</sup> Because random neurons are removed during each training step, no neuron can rely on specific other neurons being present, which prevents groups of neurons from "co-adapting" to memorize the training images. Srivastava et al. also describe a dropout network as approximately averaging the predictions of many smaller networks, and averaging tends to make predictions more stable. This could explain why the dropout models in my experiment were also more consistent across seeds.

The accuracy improvement was small, between about 0.5 and 1 percentage point. One reason is that early stopping already protected the models without dropout from the worst effects of overfitting, since each model was evaluated at its best epoch instead of after it had overfit. Early stopping is itself a form of regularization,<sup>1,5</sup> so the gains in this study are the benefit of dropout on top of early stopping rather than compared with no regularization at all. Even so, the improvement was not trivial in practical terms. With 1,000 training images, dropout 0.5 increased accuracy by 0.88 points, while doubling the training set to 2,000 images without dropout increased accuracy by 2.31 points. In other words, dropout provided roughly 40% of the benefit of doubling the data, without collecting any new images.

4.3 Effect of Training Set Size

The gain from dropout was roughly level from 1,000 to 5,000 training images and then smaller at 10,000 images, although the difference between 2,000 and 10,000 images was not statistically significant. This pattern resembles the results of Srivastava et al., who found on MNIST that the gain from dropout increased with training-set size up to a point and then declined.<sup>3</sup> They also found no improvement for extremely small training sets of 100 and 500 images, because their much larger network could still memorize the data despite dropout.<sup>3</sup> My smallest training set (1,000 images) still showed a clear benefit, so my experiment did not reach the very small sizes where dropout stopped helping in their study, possibly also because my network was much smaller than theirs.

The overfitting measures help explain the trend. Without dropout, the gap at epoch 20 fell from 12.13 points with 1,000 images to 7.36 points with 10,000 images, because a larger training set is harder to memorize. Since there is less overfitting to prevent with more data, it makes sense that dropout's benefit becomes smaller. Testing larger training sets, such as the full 60,000 images, would show whether the gain continues to shrink.

4.4 Dropout and Errors Between Similar Clothing

My original motivation was to find out whether dropout changes the kinds of mistakes a network makes, not just how many. The results suggest that it mostly does not. Dropout reduced the within-group confusion rate by about 1 percentage point at every training size, but the share of errors that stayed within the group of similar classes did not change. In other words, dropout reduced confusions between T-shirts, pullovers, coats, and shirts at about the same rate as it reduced all other mistakes. Shirt remained by far the hardest class in every condition, which agrees with Nocentini et al., who found that shirts were mostly confused with T-shirts/tops,<sup>11</sup> and the most common confusions (shirt with T-shirt/top, and pullover with coat) were the same with and without dropout. Balestriero et al. found that regularization can affect different classes very unequally.<sup>14</sup> For this group of four classes, I did not observe such an effect: dropout reduced their confusions at about the same rate as other mistakes, although I did not analyze every class in detail.

One explanation is that these confusions are not mainly caused by overfitting. Shirts, T-shirts, pullovers, and coats can look very similar in 28 × 28 grayscale images, and an MLP that flattens each image into a list of pixels has no built-in way to detect local features such as collars, buttons, or sleeve shapes. If the network's difficulty with these classes comes from the limits of the model and the images rather than from memorization, then a method that reduces memorization, like dropout, would not be expected to fix them specifically. This also explains why Hypothesis 3 was not supported: I predicted dropout would reduce these confusions more with 10,000 images, but the reduction was about the same at every size. Convolutional neural networks (CNNs), which are designed to detect local visual features,<sup>10</sup> may be better suited to these errors. However, previous studies found that shirts, T-shirts, pullovers, and coats remain the most confused categories even for CNNs that reach over 90% accuracy,<sup>11,12</sup> which suggests that part of the difficulty comes from the low-resolution images themselves.

The pilot study had suggested that dropout made the network avoid predicting "shirt," but this did not appear in the final experiment. The pilot measured each model at a single final epoch while its accuracy was still changing by several points from one epoch to the next, so that pattern was most likely caused by the instability of undertrained models rather than by dropout. This shows why repeated runs and stable model selection are important before drawing conclusions about specific error patterns.<sup>15</sup>

4.5 Measuring Overfitting

Hypothesis 2 was not supported by the measure I planned in advance, even though the exploratory measures showed a strong effect. The planned measure compared each model at the epoch it was selected, but early stopping selected the models without dropout before they had overfit much, while models with dropout were selected much later. As a result, the planned measure compared models at very different points in training. I report this openly because it is a limitation of my analysis plan rather than evidence against dropout's effect on overfitting. A future study should plan to measure overfitting at matched epochs from the start.

4.6 Could the Learning Rate Explain the Results?

The models with dropout used a learning rate of 0.003, while the models without dropout used 0.01, which raises the question of whether the improvement came from the lower learning rate instead of from dropout. The learning-rate pilot (Table 1) suggests it did not. Without dropout, a learning rate of 0.003 produced a slightly worse validation loss (0.4714) than 0.01 (0.4664), so giving the no-dropout model the lower learning rate would not have helped it. In addition, the dropout 0.5 models achieved a lower validation loss (0.4515) than the best no-dropout setting even when they used the same learning rate of 0.01. These pilot comparisons used only two seeds and the validation set, so they are supporting evidence rather than a formal test. Because 0.003 was the lowest learning rate I tested, it is also possible that the dropout models would have performed even better with a lower learning rate, which would make the reported gains an underestimate.
